First, here is a starter result (though not actually necessary for the proof). We prove that 
if $\textrm{Op}(a,b)=\textrm{Op}(c,d)$, then $a=c$ and $b=d$.

\begin{proof}
Suppose $\textrm{Op}(a,b)=\textrm{Op}(c,d)$. For simplicity's sake, let $S_1=\mathcal{G}\times\{a\}$, 
$S_2=\mathcal{G}\times\{a,b\}$, $S_3=\mathcal{G}\times\{c\}$ and $S_4=\mathcal{G}\times\{c,d\}$. Then 
$\textrm{Up}(a,a)=S_1$, $\textrm{Up}(a,b)=S_2$, $\textrm{Up}(c,c)=S_3$ and $\textrm{Up}(c,d)=S_4$.
Also, $\textrm{Op}(a,b)=\textrm{Up}(S_1,S_2)=\mathcal{G}\times\{S_1,S_2\}$ and 
$\textrm{Op}(c,d)=\textrm{Up}(S_3,S_4)=\mathcal{G}\times\{S_3,S_4\}$.

Because $\mathcal{G}\times\{S_1,S_2\}=\mathcal{G}\times\{S_3,S_4\}$, it 
follows that either $S_1=S_3$ or $S_1=S_4$, and either $S_2=S_4$ or $S_1=S_3$. 
If the former is immediately the case, we are done. On the 
other hand, suppose that $S_1=S_4$. On the one hand, suppose that $S_2=S_3$. Then it is easily 
seen to follow that $f=h$. From this, and the assumption that $S_1=S_3$, it also 
can easily be seen to follow that $f=k$; similarly, because $S_2=S_3$, $g=h$ also easily follows. 
Then it can easily be concluded that $S_1=S_2=S_3=S_4$. On the other hand, suppose that $S_2=S_4$. If also 
$S_2=S_3$, then the result follows by the above reasoning. If, on the other hand, $S_2\ne S_3$, it immediately 
follows (because either $S_3=S_1$ or $S_3=S_2$) that $S_1=S_3$, which also completes the proof. 

\end{proof}
