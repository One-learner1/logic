\subsection*{Exercise 2.1}
To prove the exercise, we first prove the following useful lemma.

\begin{lemma}
Suppose two classes $A$ and $B$ are $\in$-isomorphic under a function $\pi$. Then, for any subclass $C$ of $A$, if 
a class $D$ is definable over $C$, then $\pi''(D)$ is definable over $\pi''(C)$. In particular, if a formula 
$\phi$ (whose constants are $a_1,...,a_n$) defines $D$ over $C$, then $\psi$, which is the result of replacing 
each $a_i$ in $\phi$ by $\pi(a_i)$, defines $\pi''(D)$ over $\pi''(C)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Assume the hypothesis. Consider any subclass $C$ of $A$. We prove this by induction on the degree of the formulas.

Atomic case: In this case, the formula would have to either be of the form $x\in a$ or $a\in x$, where $a\in C$. 
Suppose that it is the first case; that is, the formula is of the form $x\in a$ (where $a\in C$). We show that 
$\pi''(D)$ is definable over $\pi''(C)$ by $x\in\pi(a)$. Well, note that $\pi(a)$ is indeed a member of 
$\pi''(C)$ (as $a\in C$). On the one hand, suppose that $\pi(b)\in\pi(a)$ holds for some $\pi(b)\in\pi''(C)$ (where $b\in C$). 
Then, because $\pi$ is an $\in$-isomorphism, $b\in a$, meaning $b\in D$, and thus $\pi(b)\in\pi''(D)$. On the other hand, 
suppose that $\pi(b)\in\pi''(D)$ (for some $b\in D$). Then $b\in D$, meaning $b\in a$, and thus, because $\pi$ is an 
$\in$-isomorphism, $\pi(b)\in\pi(a)$. Thus $x\in\pi(a)$ defines $\pi''(D)$ over $\pi''(C)$, therefore completing the first case. The second 
case (when the formula is of the form $a\in x$) is nearly identical.

Negation case: In this case, suppose the result holds for some formula $\phi(x)$, and that $\oldneg\phi(x)$ defines 
$D$ over $C$. Suppose also that the only constants in $\phi(x)$ are $a_1,...,a_n$. We show that 
$\oldneg\psi(x)$, where $\psi$ is the result of replacing each $a_i$ in $\phi$ by $\pi(a_i)$, defines 
$\pi''(D)$ over $\pi''(C)$. First note that all constants in $\oldneg\psi$ are in $\pi''(C)$ (as 
each constant is of the form $\pi(a_i)$ where $a_i\in C$). On the one hand, suppose that 
$\oldneg\psi(\pi(a))$ holds for some $\pi(a)\in\pi''(C)$ (where $a\in C$). By assumption, it follows that 
$\pi(a)\in\pi''(D)$ (because $\phi(x)$ defines $C-D$, and because $\oldneg\psi(\pi(a))$ is true, we know that 
$\psi(\pi(a))$ is false, hence $\pi(a)\in\pi''(C)$ but $\pi(a)\notin\pi''(C)-\pi''(D)$, meaning $\pi(a)\in\pi''(D)$).

On the other hand, if $\pi(a)\in\pi''(D)$ (for some $a\in D$), then $a\in D$, meaning 
$\oldneg\psi(\pi(a))$ is true over $\pi''(C)$ (by assumption of $\phi$).

Conjunction case: In this case, suppose that the result holds for some formulas $\phi$ (with constants $a_1,...,a_n$, and which 
defines some class $K$) and $\psi$
(with constants $b_1,...,b_n$, and which defines some class $L$), and that the formula
$\gamma(x)$: $\phi\wedge\psi$ defines $D$ over $C$. Let $\phi'$ and $\psi'$ be the results of replacing the occurences of all
$a_i$ in $\phi$ with $\pi(a_i)$, and the occurences of all $b_i$ in $\psi$ with $\pi(b_i)$, respectively. Then let 
$\gamma'(x)$ be $\phi'\wedge\psi'$. Then, of course, all constants of $\gamma'(x)$ are in $\pi''(C)$.

On the one hand, suppose that $\gamma'(\pi(a))$ holds for some $\pi(a)\in\pi''(C)$. Then either 
$\phi'(\pi(a))$ or $\psi'(\pi(a))$ is true over $\pi''(C)$. Suppose that $\phi'(\pi(a))$ is true over 
$\pi''(C)$ (the case where $\psi'(\pi(a))$ is true over $\pi''(C)$ is identical). Then $\pi(a)\in\pi''(K)$ (by assumption), hence 
$a\in K$, hence $\phi(a)$ is true over $C$, hence $\gamma(a)$ is true over $C$, hence $a\in D$, and therefore 
$\pi(a)\in\pi''(D)$.

On the other hand, suppose that $\pi(a)\in\pi''(D)$. Then $a\in D$, and hence either $\phi(a)$ or $\psi(a)$ is true over 
$C$. Without loss of generality, suppose that $\phi(a)$ is true over $C$. Then $\phi'(\pi(a))$ is true over $\pi''(C)$ (by assumption), 
meaning $\gamma'(\pi(a))$ is also true over $\pi''(C)$. 

Existential case: In this case, consider some formula of the form $(\exists x)\phi(x,y)$ (with constants $a_1,...,a_n$) which 
defines some class $D$ over $C$. Let $\phi'(x,y)$ be the result of replacing each occurence of a $a_i$ in $\phi(x,y)$ with 
$\pi(a_i)$. Then, of course, all constants of $(\exists x)\phi'(x,y)$ are in $\pi''(C)$. 

On the one hand, suppose that $(\exists x)\phi'(x,\pi(a))$ holds for some $\pi(a)\in\pi''(C)$. Then, for some 
$\pi(b)\in\pi''(C)$, $\phi'(\pi(b),\pi(a))$ is true over $\pi''(C)$, meaning, by assumption (applied to $\phi(b,x)$),
$\phi(b,a)$ is true over $C$, hence $(\exists x)\phi(x,a)$ is true over $C$, hence $a\in D$, hence $\pi(a)\in\pi''(D)$.

On the other hand, suppose that $\pi(a)\in\pi''(D)$. Then $a\in D$, hence 
$\phi(b,a)$ is true over $C$ for some $b\in C$. Then, by assumption (applied to $\phi(b,x)$), 
it follows that $\phi'(\pi(b),\pi(a))$ is true over $\pi''(C)$, hence $(\exists x)(x,\pi(a))$ is true over 
$\pi''(C)$.
\end{proof}

Now we can prove Exercise 2.1. That is, we suppose that $(K_0,K_1,...,K_\alpha,...)$ $(\alpha<\gamma)$ is a pseudo-construction 
and that $K=\cup_\alpha<\gamma K_\alpha$, and that $\pi$ is a Mostowski-Shpepherdson map on $K$. We prove that

(a) For each $\alpha<\gamma$, $\pi''(K_\alpha)=L_\alpha$.

(b) $\pi''(K)=L_\gamma$

\begin{proof}

(a) By transfinite induction.

Least ordinal case: This is given by $C_1$.

Successor case: Suppose the result holds for some $\alpha<\lambda$ (and that $\alpha^+<\lambda$), that is, 
$\pi''(K_\alpha)=L_\alpha$. We must show that $\pi''(K_{\alpha^+})=L_{\alpha^+}=\mathcal{F}(L_\alpha)$.

On the one hand, suppose that $x\in\pi''(K_{\alpha^+})$. Then $x=\pi(a)$ for some 
$a\in K_{\alpha^+}$. By $C_2$, $a\cap K\in\mathcal{F}(K_\alpha)$. Also, because 
$\pi$ is a Mostowski-Shepherdson map, $\pi(a)=\pi''(a\cap K)$. By the above lemma, it follows that 
$\pi''(a\cap K)$ is definable over $\pi''(K_\alpha)=L_\alpha$, meaning $\pi''(a\cap K)\in\mathcal{F}(L_\alpha)=L_{\alpha^+}$, 
that is, $\pi(a)\in L_{\alpha^+}$. Hence $\pi''(K_{\alpha^+})\subseteq L_{\alpha^+}$.

On the other hand, suppose that $x\in L_{\alpha^+}=\mathcal{F}(L_\alpha)$. By the above lemma, it follows that 
$(\pi^{-1})''(x)\in\mathcal{F}(K_\alpha)$. Thus (by $C_2$), for some $a\in K_{\alpha+1}$, 
$a\cap K=(\pi^{-1})''(x)$. Then $\pi(a)\in\pi''(K_{\alpha+1})$. But $\pi(a)=\pi''(a\cap K)$ (because 
$\pi$ is a Mostowski-Shepherdson mapping) and 
$\pi''(a\cap K)=\pi''((\pi^{-1})''(x))=x$, and thus $x\in\pi''(K_{\alpha+1})$. Thus 
$L_{\alpha^+1}\subseteq\pi''(K_{\alpha+1})$.

Hence we have shown that $\pi''(K_{\alpha^+}=L_{\alpha^+}$.

Limit ordinal case: This is obvious (by $C_3$).

(b) Obvious (using part (a) of this exercise).
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Exercise 2.2}
Suppose $K$ and $M$ are sets, and $K$ is bound up with $M$. We prove that $\mathcal{F}(K\cap M)$ is the set of all 
elements $a\cap K$ such that $a\in (K\cap\mathcal{F}(M))$.

\begin{proof}

On the one hand, suppose that $x\in\mathcal{F}(K\cap M)$. Because $K\cap M$ is Henkin closed with respct to $M$ (by $B_1$), it follows that 
$x\in\mathcal{F}(M)$; more specifically, $x$ is definable over $M$ by a formula whose constants are all in $K\cap M$ (this is just the same 
formula that defines $x$ over $K\cap M$). By $B_2$, it follows that $x\in K$. Thus $x\in (K\cap\mathcal{F}(M))$. 
But also $x=x\cap K$ (because all elements of $x$ are in $K\cap M$, hence in $K$). Hence there is some $a$ (namely $x$ itself) such that 
$x=a\cap K$ and $a\in (K\cap\mathcal{F}(M))$.

On the other hand, suppose that $x=a\cap K$ where $a\in (K\cap\mathcal{F}(M))$. By $B_2$, it follows that $a$ is definable over 
$M$ by a formula $\phi$ whose constants are all in $K\cap M$. Because $K\cap M$ is Henkin closed with respect to $M$, it follows that 
$\phi$ defines $a\cap K$ over $K\cap M$, meaning $a\cap K\in\mathcal{F}(K\cap M)$. 

Thus we have shown that $\mathcal{F}(K\cap M)$ is the set of all elements $a\cap K$ such that $a\in (K\cap\mathcal{F}(M))$.

\end{proof}

We are to show that the $\gamma$-tuple $(K_0,...,K_\alpha,...)$ ($\alpha<\gamma$) is a pseudo-construction (of $K$).

$C_2$: Consider any $\alpha<\gamma$. Then $K$ is bound up with $L_{\overline{\alpha}}$. Also, 
$K_{\alpha+1}=K\cap L_{\overline{\alpha+1}}$, $K_\alpha=K\cap L_{\overline{\alpha}}$. Note that 
$L_{\overline{\alpha+1}}=\mathcal{F}(L_{\overline{\alpha}})$ (because $\overline{\alpha}+1\in o(K)$, meaning

$C_3$: Consider some limit ordinal $\delta<\gamma$. Then $K_\delta=K\cap L_{\overline{\delta}}$. We are to show that

Hence $x\in K\cap L_{\overline{\beta}}$, meaning $x\in K_\beta$, for some $\beta<\delta$.

\subsection*{Exercise 2.4}
We must show that $K$ is extensional, $B$-closed, and of the same cardinality as $A$.

(a) We first show that $K$ is extensional. Consider any $x,y$ in $K$ such that $x\ne y$. Then there must be some $n$ such that 
both $x$ and $y$ are in $A_n$. Then there is some element of $(x-y)\cup (y-x)$ in $A_{n+1}$, hence in $K$, meaning 
$K$ is extensional.

(b) We next show that $K$ is $B$-closed. We must therefore show that $K$ is bound up with $L_\alpha$ for every $\alpha\in o(K)$. Hence, for 
every such $\alpha$, we must show that conditions $B_1$ and $B_2$ of Exercise 2.2 hold (taking $L_\alpha$ for $M$).

$B_1$: We show that $K\cap L_\alpha$ is Henkin closed with respct to $M$. Because $\alpha\in o(K)$, it follows that 
$\alpha\in o(A_n)$ for some $n$. Consider any formula $\phi$ (whose constants are all in $B$) of the form $(\exists x)\psi(x,y_1,...,y_n)$, and 
suppose that for some elements $b_1,...,b_n$ of $K\cap L_\alpha$, there is an element $a$ of $L_\alpha$ such 
that $\psi(a,b_1,...,b_n)$ is true over $L_\alpha$. Then by 3(a), it follows that there is some 
$b\in A_{n+1}$ such that $b\in L_\alpha$ and $\psi(b,b_1,...,b_n)$ is true over $L_\alpha$. But this means there is some 
$b\in K\cap L_\alpha$ ($b\in K$ as $b\in A_{n+1}$) such that $\psi(b,b_1,...,b_n)$ is true over $L_\alpha$, therefore meaning 
$K\cap L_\alpha$ is Henkin closed with respect to $L_\alpha$.

$B_2$: Now we show that for any $a\in\mathcal{F}(L_\alpha)$, $a\in K$ iff $a$ is definable over $L_\alpha$ by a formula whose constants are all in 
$K\cap L_\alpha$. So suppose that $a\in\mathcal{F}(L_\alpha)$. On the one hand, suppose that $a\in K$. Then $a\in A_n$ for some $n$. Then, by 
$3(b_1)$ tells us that there is a formula that defines $a$ over $L_\alpha$ such that all of its constants are in $A_{n+1}$ (hence also in $K$). But 
this means that $a$ is definable over $L_\alpha$ by a formula whose constants are all in $K\cap L_\alpha$. On the other hand, suppose that 
$a$ is definable over $L_\alpha$ by a formula whose constants are all in $K\cap L_\alpha$. Then there is some $A_n$ that contains all the constants of 
that formula (because there are only finitely many constants in the formula). Hence the constants of that formula are all in $A_n\cap L_\alpha$, and thus, 
by $3(b_2)$, $a\in A_{n+1}$, hence $a\in K$.

Thus $K$ is bound up with $L_\alpha$ for every $\alpha\in o(K)$.

(c) We lastly show that $K$ has the same cardinality has $A$. Because $A$ is infinite, it suffices to show that 
$\overline{\overline{A_n}}=\overline{\overline{A}}$ for each $n$.

Obviously this holds for $A_0$.

Suppose it holds for some $A_n$. The news sets added into $A_{n+1}$ are obtained from (1), (2), $(3a)$, $(3b_1)$, and $(3b_2)$. 
Because there are only $A$ distinct pairs of $x,y$ in $A_n$ (because $A_n\times A_n\cong A$ by assumption), it follows there are at most 
$A$ elements added by (1). Next, $o(A_n)\cong A$ (as can easily be seen, because there are at most $A$ orders of the elements in $A_n$, 
and, defining $D(\alpha)$ to be $\{\alpha,\alpha+1,\alpha+2,...,\alpha+n,...,\}$ for all orders of $A_n$, we get 
$o(A_n)=\cup_{\alpha\textrm{ is the order of an element in $A_n$}}D(\alpha)$, and because each $D(\alpha)$ has cardinality at most 
$A$, it follows that $o(A_n)$ has cardinality of most $A$), meaning there are at most $A$ elements added to $A_{n+1}$ by (2).

As for $(3a)$, note that for every $\alpha\in o(A_n)$, there are only at most $A$ formulas $(\exists x)\phi(x)$ with constants in
$A_n\cap L_\alpha$, meaning there are at most $A$ elements added to $A_n$ by 3(a) for that particular $\alpha$, and thus, because there are 
only $A$ elements in $o(A_n)$, there are at most $A$ elements added to $A_n$ by 3(a) in total.

As for $(3b_1)$, for each $\alpha\in o(A_n)$, there are at most $A$ element $a$'s such that $a\in A_n$ and is definable over 
$L_\alpha$, meaning there are at most $A$ elements added by $(3b_1)$ (because for any formula defining some $a\in A_n$ over 
$L_\alpha$, it must have only a finite number of constants, and the constants are what's added to $A_{n+1}$).

Lastly, as for $(3b_2)$, for each $\alpha\in o(A_n)$, there are at most $A_n$ subsets of $L_\alpha$ that are definable over $L_\alpha$ by a formula with 
constants in $A_n\cap L_\alpha$, meaning there are at most $A$ elements added by $(3b_2)$ (because 
there are at most $A$ elements of $o(A_n)$).

Thus there are at most $A$ elements added to $A_n$, and hence $A_{n+1}$ also contains $A$ elements.

Thus we have shown that $K$ is extentional, $B$-closed, and has no higher cardinality than $A$.

\subsection*{Exercise 2.6}
Suppose that $m$ is a constructible subset of $L_c$ ($c$ infinite). Then, by Theorem GI, $L_c\cup\{m\}$ can be extended to an 
extensional set $K$ of cardinality $c$ that is Mostowski-Shepherdson mappable to some $L_\alpha$. Let $\pi$ be this 
Mostowski-Shepherdson map. We first show that $\pi(m)=m$. Well, $L_c$ is transitive, meaning, by Theorem 5.1 of Chapter 10, 
$\pi(x)=x$ for each $x\in L_c$. Then, $\pi(m)=\pi''(m)$, and, because $m$ is a subset of $L_c$, 
$\pi(m)=m$. Next we show that $\alpha<c^*$. Well, $K$ has the same cardinality as $L_c$, and $L_\alpha$ has the same 
cardinality as $K$ (because $K$ is isomorphic to $L_\alpha$), meaning $L_\alpha$ has the same cardinality as 
$L_c$. But $L_c$ has cardinality $c$, and $c^*$ is the next cardinal after $c$, meaning $L_\alpha$ has cardinality 
less than $c^*$. But $\alpha$ must be infinite, meaning $L_\alpha$ has cardinality $\alpha$, and thus 
$\alpha<c^*$, and hence $m\in L_{c^*}$.
