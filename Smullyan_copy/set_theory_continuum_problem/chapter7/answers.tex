\section*{Section 1}
In the Remarks are page 91, it is said that there is an \textquote{easy induction} showing that $R_{n+1}$ has $2^n$ elements. However, this is clearly 
not the case. For, as remakred, $R_3$ has 4 elements. Then $R_4$ has $2^4=16$ elements, which is not equal to $2^3=8$. 

The proof of Proposition 1.3 is problematic. It uses Cantor's Theorem, though this hasn't even been stated (let alone proven) in the context of 
Zermelo-Frankel Universes. Proposition 1.3 can also be proven without using Cantor's Theorem. In particular, we first show that $R_\alpha\not\in R_\alpha$.

\begin{proof}
By transfinite induction. Clearly this holds for 0. Suppose it holds for some $\alpha$. Then, if $R_{\alpha+1}\in R_{\alpha+1}$, by definition 
of $R_{\alpha+1}$, $R_{\alpha+1}$ would be a subset of $R_\alpha$. But $R_\alpha\in R_{\alpha+1}$. Thus this would mean that 
$R_\alpha\in R_\alpha$, contradicting the induction assumption. Hence $R_{\alpha+1}\notin R_{\alpha+1}$. On the other hand, suppose it held for all 
ordinals less than some limit ordinal $\lambda$. Then, by definition, $R_\lambda=\cup_{\alpha<\lambda}R_\alpha$. Suppose 
$R_\lambda\in R_\lambda$. Then, for some $\alpha<\lambda$, $R_\lambda\in R_\alpha$. But $R_{\alpha+1}\subset R_\lambda$, and 
$R_\alpha\in R_{\alpha+1}$, meaning $R_\alpha\in R_\lambda$. Thus, because $R_\alpha$ is transitive, $R_\alpha\in R_\alpha$, contradicting the 
induction assumption. Thus $R_\lambda\notin R_\lambda$, completing the transfinite induction.
\end{proof}

Then, in the proof of Proposition 1.3, $R_\alpha\ne R_{\alpha+1}$ follows for the following reason: We know that 
$R_\alpha\in R_{\alpha+1}$ and $R_\alpha\notin R_\alpha$ (by the above result), meaning 
$R_\alpha\ne R_{\alpha+1}$.

Here is why if $C$ is a class of ordinals such that there is no ordinal greater than all ordinals in $C$, then $C$ cannot be a set. For suppose it were a 
set. Then $C$ would be a chain of ordinals, hence $\cup C$ would be an ordinal. But $\cup C=On$. For obviously $\cup C\subseteq On$. On the other hand, for any 
ordinal $\alpha$, there must be some ordinal $\beta\in C$ such that $\alpha<\beta$, meaning $\alpha\in\cup C$. Thus this would mean that $On$ was an ordinal, which 
is impossible. 


Note that if $x$ has rank and $y\subseteq x$, then $y$ also has rank. Say $x$ has rank $\alpha$. Then $x\subseteq R_\alpha$, meaning 
all elements of $x$ are elements of $R_\alpha$, hence have rank. Now, $x$ is a set, so $y$ is also a set. Thus $y$ is a set of elements which 
have rank, meaning, by $Q_4$, $y$ has rank. 
 
Here is why $\omega\times 2$ is the least limit ordinal $>\omega$. For suppose we had some limit ordinal $\lambda$ such that 
$\omega<\lambda<\omega\times 2$. Thus, for some $x\in\omega\times 2$, $\lambda\in\omega\times 2$. By definition of 
$\omega\times 2$, either $\lambda$ is a natural number, or $\lambda=\omega+n$ for some natural number $n$. The former case is ruled out because 
$\lambda$ is a limit ordinal. Because $\lambda\ne\omega$ by assumption, this would mean that $\lambda=\omega+n$ for some $n>0$. But this would mean that 
$\lambda$ was a successor ordinal, a contradiction. Hence there can be no limit ordinal $\lambda$ such that $\omega<\lambda<\omega\times 2$.

In the proof of Theorem 4.1, here is why $\omega\in R_\lambda$ (if $\lambda>\omega$ and $\lambda$ is a limit ordinal). This is because $R_{\omega^+}\subset R_\lambda$, and 
$\omega\in R_{\omega^+}$.

In the proof of Theorem 4.2, here is why, if $Z$ is a Zermelo universe and if $\beta\in Z$, then $\beta^+\in Z$. For suppose $\beta\in Z$. Then, because $Z$ is a Zermelo universe, 
$\{\beta\}\in Z$, and also $\{\beta,\{\beta\}\}\in Z$, and thus also $\cup\{\beta,\{\beta\}\}\in Z$. But

Certinaly, we can show that for a Zermelo-Fraenkel universe $U$, $U\notin U$.

\begin{proof}
For suppose $U\in U$. Then $\mathcal{P}(U)\in U$, meaning $\mathcal{P}(U)\subseteq U$, hence

Actually, we can show that every Zermelo-Fraenkel universe $U$ must contain all ordinals of $V$.

\begin{proof}
By superinduction. Obviously $\emptyset\in U$. Also, if 
$\alpha\in U$, then $\alpha^+=\cup\{\alpha,\{\alpha\}\}$, meaning, because $U$ is a 
Zermelo-Fraenkel universe, that $\alpha^+\in U$. Now suppose some chain $C$ of ordinals 
is in $U$. Then, because $U$ is a Zermelo-Franekel universe, $\cup C\in U$.
\end{proof}

\end{proof}

\subsection*{Exercise 4.1}
We show that $R_\Omega$ is the intersection of all subclasses $C$ of $V$ such that $C$ is a Zermelo-Fraenkel universe containing all ordinals of $V$.

(1) On the one hand, suppose that $x\in R_\Omega$. We need to show that $x$ is in the intersection of all subclasses $C$ of $V$ such that $C$ is a 
Zermelo-Fraenkel universe containing all ordinals of $V$. So consider any sublcass $C$ of $V$ such that $C$ is a 
Zermelo-Fraenkel universe containing all ordinals of $V$.

We first show that $R_\alpha\in C$ for all $\alpha$. Becuase $R_0=\emptyset$, and $\emptyset\in C$, $R_0\in C$. 
Suppose now for some $\alpha$, $R_\alpha\in C$. Then $R_{\alpha^+}=\mathcal{P}(R_\alpha)$. But, by the power axiom, 
$\mathcal{P}(R_\alpha)\in C$, meaning $R_{\alpha^+}\in C$. On the other hand, suppose that $R_\alpha\in C$ for all 
$\alpha<\lambda$ for a limit ordinal $\lambda$. Then $R_\lambda=\cup_{\alpha<\lambda}R_\alpha$. Now, $\lambda\in C$ by assumption, 
and the the set of all $R_\alpha$ for $\alpha<\lambda$ can be put into a 1-1 correspondence with $\lambda$, hence is in $C$ (by the axiom of 
substitution). Thus $\cup_{\alpha<\lambda}R_\alpha=R_\lambda\in C$. Hence, for all ordinals $\alpha$, $R_\alpha\in C$.

Having shown this, because $x\in R_\Omega$, $x\in R_{\alpha+1}$ for some $\alpha$. But $R_{\alpha+1}\in C$, and because 
$C$ is supercomplete, $R_{\alpha+1}\subset C$, meaning $x\in C$. Thus we have shown that $R_\Omega$ is a subset 
of the intersection of all subclasses $C$ of $V$ such that $C$ is a Zermel-Fraenkel universe containing all ordinals of $V$.

On the other hand, suppose that $x$ is in the intersetion of all subclasses $C$ of $V$ such that $C$ is a Zermelo-Fraenkel universe containing all ordinals of $V$. Well, 
$R_\Omega$ is a subclass of $V$ that is a Zermelo-Fraeknel universe containing all ordinals (by $P_7$ and Theorem 4.1), meaning $x\in R_\Omega$. Hence 
the intersection of all subclasses $C$ of $V$ such that $C$ is a Zermelo-Fraeknel universe containing all ordinals of $V$ is a subclass of $R_\Omega$. 

Consequently, $R_\Omega$ is the intersection of all subclasses $C$ of $V$ such that $C$ is a Zermelo-Fraenkel universe containing all ordinals of $V$.

\subsection*{Exercise 4.2}
I did not know what a Cauchy sequence was. Instead of solving the exact problem, I proved that the set of all rationals is a set with rank.

First, every fraction is a set with rank. For consider some fraction $\langle m,n\rangle$. Then $m$ and $n$ are both ordinals. Without loss of generality, suppose that 
$m\ge n$. Thus $m\in R_{m+1}$ and $n\in R_{m+1}$. Now, $\langle m,n\rangle=\{\{m\},\{m,n\}\}$. Thus 
$\langle m,n\rangle\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(R_{m+1}))$, that is, $\langle m,n\rangle\in R_{m+3}$, hence $\langle m,n\rangle$ has rank.

Now consider $\mathbb{F}$. Clearly $\mathbb{F}$ does not have rank $n$ for a natural number $n$. We show that $\mathbb{F}$ has rank 
$\omega$. That is, we show that $\langle m,n\rangle\in R_\omega$ for all positive integers $m$ and $n$, which would mean that 
$\mathbb{F}\subseteq R_\omega$. 

Consider any $\langle m,n\rangle$, that is, $\{\{m\},\{m,n\}\}$. Without loss of generality, let $m$ be the maximum of $m,n$. 
Then $m\in R_{m+1}$ and $n\in R_{m+1}$. Hence $\{\{m\},\{m,n\}\}\in R_{m+3}$, meaning (by the definition of $R_\omega$) that 
$\langle m,n\rangle\in R_\omega$. Thus $\mathbb{F}$ has rank $\omega$.

For our purposes, let $\mathbb{Q}$ be the set of all sets of fractions, that is, $\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{F})$. We show that 
$\mathbb{Q}$ has rank $\omega+1$. Clearly $\mathbb{Q}\subseteq R_{\omega+1}$ (because 
$R_{\omega+1}=\mathcal{P}(R_\omega)$ and $\mathbb{F}\subseteq R_\omega$, and thus 
$\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{F})\subseteq \mathcal{P}(R_\omega)$). Clearly $\mathbb{Q}$ does not have rank $n$ where $n$ is a natural number. 
Then the only possiblity other than $\omega+1$ would be $\omega$. But by $P_6$, this would mean 
(because $\mathbb{F}\in\mathbb{Q}$) that $\mathbb{F}$ had rank lower than $\omega$, contradicting the fact that $\mathbb{F}$ has rank 
$\omega$. Hence $\mathbb{Q}$ does not have rank $\omega$, and consequently, $\mathbb{Q}$ has rank $\omega+1$.
