\section*{Problem 1}
\textit{Note}: Here is why the diagonal function is implied by the iteration theorem. 
Consider any $R_y(x,y,z)$. By the iteration theorem, there is some $\phi(y,z)$ such that 
$R_y(x,y,z)$ iff $R_{\phi(y,z)}(x)$. By definition of $R^n_i(x_1,...,x_n)$ when $n=1$, $R_{\phi(y,z)}(x)$ means $x\in\omega_{\phi(y,z)}$, and thus 
$x\in\omega_{\phi(y,z)}$ iff $R_y(x,y,z)$.


Consider any two recursively enumerable relations $M_1(x,y,z)$ and $M_2(x,y,z)$. Hence the relations $M_1(x,d(y,z),d(z,y))$ and $M_2(x,d(z,y),d(y,z))$ are 
recursively enumerable, and have indices $m$ and $n$, respectively. Thus, if $d(y,z)$ is the diagonal function:
$x\in\omega_{d(m,n)}$ iff $M_1(x,d(m,n),d(n,m))$ and $x\in\omega_{d(n,m)}$ iff $M_2(x,d(m,n),d(n,m))$, thus showing that $a=d(m,n)$ and $b=d(n,m)$ satisfies the 
theorem.

\section*{Problem 2}
Consider any two recursively enumerable relations $M_1(x,y_1,y_2,z_1,z_2)$ and $M_2(x,y_1,y_2,z_1,z_2)$. Hence the relations 
$M_1(x,i,j,d(y,z),d(z,y))$ and $M_2(x,i,j,d(y,z),d(z,y))$ (as relations between $x,y,z,i,j$) are recursively enumerable for the diagonal function $d$. Hence, by the 
iteration theorem, there are recursive functions $t_1(i,j)$ and $t_2(i,j)$ such that for all numbers $i,j,x,y,z$:\newline
(1): $R_{t_1(i,j)}(x,y,z)$ iff $M_1(x,i,j,d(y,z),d(z,y))$;\newline
(2): $R_{t_2(i,j)}(x,y,z)$ iff $M_2(x,i,j,d(z,y),d(y,z))$.\newline
Let $\phi_1(i,j)$ be the function $d(t_1(i,j),t_2(i,j))$ and $\phi_2(i,j)$ be the function $d(t_2(i,j),t_1(i,j))$. Thus
$x\in\omega_{\phi_1(i,j)}$ iff $R_{t_1(i,j)}(x,t_1(i,j),t_2(i,j))$ iff $M_1(x,i,j,d(t_1(i,j),t_2(i,j)),d(t_2(i,j),t_1(i,j)))$ iff 
$M_1(x,i,j,\phi_1(i,j),\phi_2(i,j))$ and 
$x\in\omega_{\phi_2(i,j)}$ iff $R_{t_2(i,j)}(x,t_2(i,j),t_1(i,j))$ iff $M_2(x,i,j,d(t_1(i,j),t_2(i,j)),d(t_2(i,j),t_1(i,j)))$ iff $M_2(x,i,j,\phi_1(i,j),\phi_2(i,j))$.
Thus $\omega_{\phi_1(i,j)}=\{x:M_1(x,i,j,\phi_1(i,j),\phi_2(i,j))\}$ and $\omega_{\phi_2(i,j)}=\{x:M_2(x,i,j,\phi_1(i,j),\phi_2(i,j))\}$, thus completing the proof.

\section*{Problem 3}
(a): Let $R_1(x,y,y_1,z_1,z_2)$ be $M_1(x,y,z_1,z_2)$, and $R_2(x,y,y_1,z_1,z_2)$ be $M_2(x,y_1,z_1,z_2)$. By Theorem 2, there are recursive functions
$\omega_{\phi_1(i,j)}$ and $\omega_{\phi_2(i,j)}$ such that:\newline
(1) $\omega_{\phi_1(i,j)}=\{x:R_1(x,i,j,\phi_1(i,j),\phi_2(i,j))\}=\{x:M_1(x,i,\phi_1(i,j),\phi_2(i,j))\}$;\newline
(2) $\omega_{\phi_2(i,j)}=\{x:R_2(x,i,j,\phi_1(i,j),\phi_2(i,j))\}=\{x:M_2(x,j,\phi_1(i,j),\phi_2(i,j))\}$\newline thus completing the proof of Theorem 3.


(b): Let $M(x,y,z_1,z_2)$ be the relation $R_y(x,z_1,z_2)$ (as a relation between $x,y,z_1,z_2)$. 
Thus, by Theorem 3, there are recursive functions $\phi_1(i,j)$ and $\phi_2(i,j)$ such that:\newline
(1) $\omega_{\phi_1(i,j)}=\{x:R_i(x,\phi_1(i,j),\phi_2(i,j))\}$;\newline
(2) $\omega_{\phi_2(i,j)}=\{x:R_j(x,\phi_1(i,j),\phi_2(i,j))\}$\newline thus showing that Theorem 4 is a special case of Theorem 3.

\section*{Problem 4}
Consider two recursively enumerable sets $A$ and $B$. They are both $\Sigma_1$ and so there are $\Sigma_0$ relations $R_1(x,y)$ and $R_2(x,y)$ such that 
for all $x$:\newline
(1) $x\in A$ iff $\exists yR_1(x,y)$;\newline
(2) $x\in B$ iff $\exists yR_2(x,y)$.\newline
Let the sets $A'$ and $B'$ be defined by the following two conditions:\newline
(1) $x\in A'$ iff $\exists y(R_1(x,y)\wedge\left(\forall z\le y\right)\oldneg R_2(x,z))$;\newline
(2) $x\in B'$ iff $\exists y(R_2(x,y)\wedge\left(\forall z\le y\right)\oldneg R_1(x,z))$.\newline
Now suppose that $x\in A'$. Thus for some $m$: 
\[ \textrm{(1) }R_1(x,m)\wedge (\forall z\le m)\oldneg R_2(x,z) \] 
Suppose that $x\in B'$. Thus for some $n$: 
\[ \textrm{(2) }R_2(x,n)\wedge (\forall z\le n)\oldneg R_1(x,z) \] 
If $m\ge n$, then $\oldneg R_2(x,n)$ by (1), which is a contradiction. If $m<n$, then $\oldneg R_1(x,m)$ by (2), which is a contradiction. Thus if 
$x\in A'$, $x\notin B'$, and if $x\in B'$ then $x\notin A'$, 
thus proving that $A'$ and $B'$ are disjoint.

\section*{Problem 5}
(1): Consider some disjoint pair of sets $(A,B)$. 
We first assume that for any disjoint recursively enumerable supsets $\omega_i$ and $\omega_j$ of $A$ and $B$ respectively, there is a number $n$ outside both 
$\omega_i$ and $\omega_j$. Now suppose that $(A,B)$ was recursively separable under the first definition. Thus there is some recursive superset $S$ of $A$ disjoint from 
$B$. Thus both $S$ and $\bar{S}$ are recursively enumerable, and $\bar{S}$ is a superset of $B$. Thus $S$ and $\bar{S}$ are two recursively enumerable supersets of 
$A$ and $B$ respectively, but there are no numbers $n$ outside both $S$ and $\bar{S}$ (because they are complements), which is a contradiction. Thus $(A,B)$ must be 
recursively inseparable under the first definition.


(2): Now suppose that $(A,B)$ was recursively inseparable under the first definition. Hence there is no recursive superset of $A$ disjoint from $B$. Now suppose that 
there were two disjoint recursively enumerable supersets $\omega_i$ and $\omega_j$ of $A$ and $B$ respectively, such that there was not a number $n$ outside both 
$\omega_i$ and $\omega_j$. Hence $\omega_i=\bar{\omega_j}$. Thus $\omega_i$ is a recursive superset of $A$ disjoint from $B$, yielding a contradiction. Hence 
$(A,B)$ must also be recursively inseparable under the second definition. Thus the proof that the two definitions are equivalent is done.

\section*{Problem 6}
Suppose that $k(x,y)$ is a Kleene function for a disjoint pair $(A_1,A_2)$. Thus:\newline
(1) If $k(i,j)\in\omega_i-\omega_j$, then $k(i,j)\in A_2$;\newline
(2) If $k(i,j)\in\omega_j-\omega_i$, then $k(i,j)\in A_1$.\newline
Suppose that $\omega_i$ and $\omega_j$ are recursively enumerable supersets of $A$ and $B$ respectively. If $k(i,j)\in\omega_i$, then if 
$k(i,j)\notin\omega_j$, $k(i,j)\in A_2$ by (1). But this is impossible, as $A_2\subseteq\omega_j$. Thus $k(i,j)\in\omega_j$. Now if $k(i,j)\in\omega_j$, then if 
$k(i,j)\notin\omega_i$, $k(i,j)\in A_1$ by (2). But this is impossible, as $A_1\subseteq\omega_i$. Thus $k(i,j)\in\omega_i$. Hence $k(i,j)\in\omega_i$ iff
$k(i,j)\in\omega_j$, showing that $(A_1,A_2)$ is completely effectively inseparable under $k(x,y)$.

\section*{Problem 7}
Let $A_1$ be the set of all numbers $\delta(i,j)$ such that $\delta(i,j)\in\omega_j$ (hence $A_1$ is recursively enumerable, in particular $x\in A_1$ iff
$\exists i\exists j(\delta(i,j)=x\wedge x\in\omega_j))$. Let $A_2$ be the set of all $\delta(i,j)$ such that 
$\delta(i,j)\in\omega_i$ (hence $A_2$ is recursively enumerable, in particular $x\in A_2$ iff $\exists i\exists j(\delta(i,j)=x\wedge x\in\omega_i)$). Now suppose that 
$\delta(i,j)\in\omega_i-\omega_j$. Thus $x\in A_2-A_1$. If $\delta(i,j)\in\omega_j-\omega_i$, then $x\in A_1-A_2$. Thus $\delta(i,j)$ is a Kleene function for $(A_2-A_1,A_1-A_2)$. 
By the separation principle, we have two disjoint recursively enumerable sets $K_1$ and $K_2$ such that $A_2-A_1\subseteq K_1$ and $A_1-A_2\subseteq K_2$. 
Thus, if $\delta(i,j)\in\omega_i-\omega_j$, 
$x\in K_2$, and if $\delta(i,j)\in\omega_j-\omega_i$, then $\delta(i,j)\in K_1$. Hence $\delta(x,y)$ is a Kleene function for the disjoint pair $(K_1,K_2)$ of recursively enumerable sets.


Here is the more general result that if $\zeta(x,y)$ is a Kleene function for $(B_1,B_2)$, and if $B_1',B_2'$ are disjoint supersets of $B_1,B_2$ respectively, then $\zeta(x,y)$ must be 
a Kleene function for $(B_1',B_2')$. So suppose that $\zeta(x,y)$ is a Kleene function for $(B_1,B_2)$, and that $B_1',B_2'$ are disjoint supersets of $B_1,B_2$ respectively. Thus:\newline
(1) If $\zeta(i,j)\in\omega_i-\omega_j$, then $\zeta(i,j)\in B_2$ and thus also in $B_2'$;\newline
(2) If $\zeta(i,j)\in\omega_j-\omega_i$, then $\zeta(i,j)\in B_1$ and thus also in $B_1'$.\newline
Hence $\zeta(i,j)$ is a Kleene function for $(B_1',B_2')$.

\section*{Problem 8}
Suppose that $f(x)$ is the function which semi-reduces $(A_1,A_2)$ to $(B_1,B_2)$. Thus:\newline
(1) If $x\in A_1$, then $f(x)\in B_1$;\newline
(2) If $x\in A_2$, then $f(x)\in B_2$.\newline
Let $k(x,y)$ be a Kleene function for $(A_1,A_2)$. Thus:\newline
(1') If $k(i,j)\in\omega_i-\omega_j$, then $k(i,j)\in A_2$;\newline
(2') If $k(i,j)\in\omega_j-\omega_i$, then $k(i,j)\in A_1$.\newline
By the iteration theorem, there is a recursive function $t(y)$ such that $x\in\omega_{t(y)}$ iff $f(x)\in\omega_y$. We now consider the function $f(k(t(i),t(j)))$.
If $f(k(t(i),t(j)))\in\omega_i-\omega_j$, then $k(t(i),t(j))\in\omega_{t(i)}-\omega_{t(j)}$, meaning, by (1'), $k(t(i),t(j))\in A_2$. By (2), $f(k(t(i)),t(j))\in B_2$. 
If $f(k(t(i),t(j)))\in\omega_j-\omega_i$, then $k(t(i),t(j))\in\omega_{t(j)}-\omega_{t(i)}$, meaning, by (2'), $k(t(i),t(j))\in A_1$. By (1), $f(k(t(i),t(j)))\in B_1$. Thus we have shown that 
the function $f(k(t(i),t(j)))$ is a Kleene function for $(B_1,B_2)$.

\section*{Problem 9}
Consider some disjoint semi-doubly-universal pair $(U_1,U_2)$. We know that some Kleene pair exists, namely $(K_1,K_2)$. Thus, by Proposition 2, because $(K_1,K_2)$ is semi-reducible to 
$(U_1,U_2)$ and $U_1$ and $U_2$ are disjoint, $(U_1,U_2)$ is a Kleene pair. Then, by Proposition 1, $(U_1,U_2)$ is completely effectively inseparable.

\section*{Problem 10}
Suppose that $(A,B)$ are doubly generative under some recursive function $\phi(x,y)$. Thus:\newline
(1) $\phi(i,j)\in A$ iff $\phi(i,j)\in\omega_i$;\newline
(2) $\phi(i,j)\in B$ iff $\phi(i,j)\in\omega_j$.\newline
Consider some index $e$ of the empty set. Now, we can see that the empty set has some index in many ways. For example, 
consider the sentence $P'\vdash P''$ having a Gödel number of $p$. Obvsioulsy, for every $x$, $px_0\notin T_0$. Hence, for $\omega_p$, because $x\in\omega_p$ iff 
$r(p,x)\in T_0$ iff $px_0\in T_0$, $\omega_p$ is the empty set, as $px_0\notin T_0$ for every $x$. Thus, because $\omega_i$ and $\omega_c$ are disjoint 
for every $i$, $\phi(i,c)\in A$ iff $\phi(i,c)\in\omega_i$. Hence $A$ is generative under $\phi(i,c)$. In addition, $B$ can be seen to be gerative under $\phi(c,i)$. 

\section*{Problem 11}
Suppose that the pair of recursively enumerable sets $(A,B)$ is completely effectively inseparable under some recursive function $\zeta(x,y)$. By the iteration theorem, there are recursive 
function $t_1(i),t_2(i)$, such that for all $x$ and $i$:\newline 
(1) $\omega_{t_1(i)}=\{x:x\in\omega_i\vee x\in A\}$;\newline
(2) $\omega_{t_2(i)}=\{x:x\in\omega_i\vee x\in B\}$.\newline
Let $\phi(i,j)=\zeta(t_1(j),t_2(i))$. Because $(A,B)$ is completely effectively inseparable under $\zeta(x,y)$, if $\omega_i$ and $\omega_j$ are recursively enumerable supersets of $A$ and 
$B$ respectively, then $\zeta(i,j)\in\omega_i$ iff $\zeta(i,j)\in\omega_j$. Now suppose that $\omega_i$ and $\omega_j$ are disjoint. 
Thus $\zeta(t_1(j),t_2(i))\in\omega_i$ iff $\zeta(t_1(j),t_2(i))\in\omega_{t_2(i)}$ (by (2)) iff $\zeta(t_1(j),t_2(i))\in\omega_{t_1(j)}$ iff $\zeta(t_1(j),t_2(i))\in A$ (by (1) and the assumed 
disjointness of $\omega_i$ and $\omega_j$). On the other hand, $\zeta(t_1(j),t_2(i))\in\omega_j$ iff $\zeta(t_1(j),t_2(i))\in\omega_{t_1(j)}$ 
(by (1)) iff $\zeta(t_1(j),t_2(i))\in\omega_{t_2(i)}$ iff $\zeta(t_1(j),t_2(i))\in B$ (by (2) and the assumed disjointness of $\omega_i$ and $\omega_j$). Thus we have shown conditions 
(1) and (2) of being doubly generative are satisfied for $(A,B)$ under the recursive function $\zeta(t_1(j),t_2(i))$.

\section*{Problem 12}
Suppose that $(A_1,A_2)$ is a disjoint pair doubly generative under the function $g(x,y)$. Thus we have conditions (1) and (2) of being doubly generative. For all numbers $i$ and $j$:


$C_1$: Suppose that $\omega_i=\N$ and $\omega_j=\emptyset$. Thus $g(i,j)\in\omega_i$, and because $\omega_i$ and $\omega_j$ are disjoint, $g(i,j)\in A_1$ by (1).\newline


$C_2$: Suppose that $\omega_i=\emptyset$ and $\omega_j=\N$. Thus $g(i,j)\in\omega_j$, and because $\omega_i$ and $\omega_j$ are disjoint, $g(i,j)\in A_2$ by (2).\newline


$C_3$: Suppose that $\omega_i=\omega_j=\emptyset$. Hence $\omega_i$ and $\omega_j$ are disjoint. Thus, if $g(i,j)\in A_1\cup A_2$, $g(i,j)\in\omega_i\cup\omega_j$ by either (1) or (2), 
contrary to the asusmption that they are the empty set. Hence $g(i,j)\notin A_1\cup A_2$.

\section*{Problem 13}
Suppose that $(A_1,A_2)$ is a disjoint pair and that $g(x,y)$ is a recursive functions such that $C_1$ and $C_2$ hold for $(A_1,A_2)$. Now consider any disjoint pair $(B_1,B_2)$ of 
recursively enumerable sets. By Problem 16 of Chapter 5, we have recursive functions $t_1(i),t_2(i)$ such that, for all $i$:\newline
(1) If $i\in B_1$, then $\omega_{t_1(i)}=\N$;\newline
(2) If $i\notin B_1$, then $\omega_{t_1(i)}=\emptyset$;\newline
and\newline
$(1')$ If $i\in B_2$, then $\omega_{t_2(i)}=\N$;\newline
$(2')$ If $i\notin B_2$, then $\omega_{t_2(i)}=\emptyset$;\newline
all hold. Let $f(x)$ be the recursive function $g(t_1(x),t_2(x))$.


(1): We first show that $(B_1,B_2)$ is semi-reducible to $(A_1,A_2)$ under $g(t_1(x),t_2(x))$. Suppose that $i\in B_1$. Thus $\omega_{t_1(i)}=\N$ by (1). If $\omega_{t_2(i)}\ne\emptyset$, then by 
$(2')$, $i\in B_2$, contrary to the assumption that $B_1$ and $B_2$ are disjoint. Thus $\omega_{t_2(i)}=\emptyset$, meaning $f(i)\in A_1$ by $C_1$. Similar reasoning can be used to prove that 
if $i\in B_2$, then $f(i)\in A_2$.


(2): Now assume that $C_3$ also holds for $g(x,y)$.
We now show that if $i\notin B_1\cup B_2$, then $i\notin A_1\cup A_2$. So suppose that $i\notin B_1\cup B_2$, meaning both $\omega_{t_1(i)}=\emptyset$ and $\omega_{t_2(i)}=\emptyset$ 
by (2) and $(2')$, respectively. Thus $g(t_1(i),t_2(i))$, that is $f(i)$, $\notin A_1\cup A_2$ by $C_3$. Thus we have shown that the third condition of $(B_1,B_2)$ being reducible to $(A_1,A_2)$ 
holds. Because we showed the first two conditions held in part (1), we can conclude that $(B_1,B_2)$ is reducible to $(A_1,A_2)$ under $g(t_1(x),t_2(x))$. 


It should be noted that the definition given for reducibility from one pair $(B_1,B_2)$ to another pair $(A_1,A_2)$ under some function $f(x)$ 
only means $B_1$ is reducible to $A_1$ and $B_2$ is reducible to $A_2$, both under $f(x)$, if 
$A_1,A_2$ are disjoint. For we are given the conditions:\newline
(1) If $x\in B_1$, then $f(x)\in A_1$;\newline
(2) If $x\in B_2$, then $f(x)\in A_2$;\newline
(3) If $x\notin B_1\cup B_2$, then $f(x)\notin A_1\cup A_2$.\newline
To show that $B_1$ is reducible to $A_1$ and $B_2$ is reducible to $A_2$, both under $f(x)$, we would need $x\in B_1$ iff $f(x)\in A_1$ and $x\in B_2$ iff $x\in A_2$. The first parts of both 
of those statements are given by (1) and (2) respectively. Suppose that $f(x)\in A_1$. By (3), it cannot be that $x\notin B_1\cup B_2$. If $x\in B_2$, then $f(x)\in A_2$, contrary to the 
assumption that $A_1$ and $A_2$ are disjoint. Thus $x\in B_1$.
Similar reasoning shows that if $f(x)\in A_2$, then $x\in B_2$. But this reasoning only works when $A_1,A_2$ are disjoint, so that assumption needs 
to be made. 

\section*{Problem 14}
Suppose that we have a doubly co-productive pair $(A,B)$ under a recursive function $g(x,y)$. Thus, for all $i$ and $j$, if $\omega_i$ is disjoint from $\omega_j$, and $\omega_i$ is disjoint 
from $A$ and $\omega_j$ disjoint from $B$, then $g(i,j)$ lies outside all the four sets $\omega_i,\omega_j,A,B$.


$D_0$: If $\omega_i=\omega_j=\emptyset$, then $\omega_i$ is disjoint from $\omega_j$, and $\omega_i$ is disjoint 
from $A$ and $\omega_j$ disjoint from $B$, meaing $g(i,j)\notin A\cup B$ by the asusmption of double co-productivity.


$D_1$: If $\omega_i=\{g(i,j)\}$ and $\omega_j=\emptyset$, then $\omega_i$ and $\omega_j$ are disjoint, and $\omega_j$ and $B$ are disjoint. If $g(i,j)\notin A$, then 
$\omega_i$ and $A$ would be disjoint, meaning, by the assumption of double co-productivity, $g(i,j)\notin\omega_i$, which is a contradiction. Thus $g(i,j)\in A$.


$D_2$: Similar reasoning can be used here as in the case of $D_1$. 

\section*{Problem 15}
Consider some recursive function $g(x,y)$. Let $M_1(x,y,z_1,z_2)$ be the recursively enumerable relation $x\in\omega_y\wedge x=g(z_1,z_2)$, and let $M_2(x,y,z_1,z_2)$ be the same relation. By 
Theorem 3, there are recursive functions $\phi_1(i,j)$ and $\phi_2(i,j)$ such that for all numbers $i$ and $j$:\newline
(1) $\omega_{\phi_1(i,j)}=\{x:M_1(x,i,\phi_1(i,j),\phi_2(i,j))\}$;\newline
(2) $\omega_{\phi_2(i,j)}=\{x:M_1(x,j,\phi_1(i,j),\phi_2(i,j))\}$.\newline
Take $t_1(i,j)$ and $t_2(i,j)$ to be $\phi_1(i,j)$ and $\phi_2(i,j)$, respectively. Thus $x\in\omega_{t_1(i,j)}$ iff $M_1(x,i,\phi_1(i,j),\phi_2(i,j))$ iff 
$x\in\omega_i\wedge x=g(\phi_1(i,j),\phi_2(i,j))$, which is the same as saying $\omega_{t_1(i,j)}=\omega_i\cap\{g(t_1(i,j),t_2(i,j))\}$. Also 
$x\in\omega_{t_2(i,j)}$ iff $M_2(x,j,\phi_1(i,j),\phi_2(i,j))$ iff $x\in\omega_j\wedge x=g(\phi_1(i,j),\phi_2(i,j))$, which is the same as saying 
$\omega_{t_2(i,j)}=\omega_j\cap\{g(t_1(i,j),t_2(i,j))\}$.
