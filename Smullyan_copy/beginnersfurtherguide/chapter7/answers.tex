\section*{Problem 1}
\textit{Note}: It should be noted that if a system is complete, then representability implies complete representability. For suppose that $A$ is 
representable by $h$ under some complete system $\mathcal{S}$. Thus $h(n)\in P$ iff $n\in A$. On the one hand, if $h'(n)\in P$ then $h(n)\in R$ by definition, and 
thus $n\notin A$, meaning $n\in\bar{A}$. If $n\in\bar{A}$, then $h(n)\notin P$, and because the system is complete, $h(n)\in R$ and thus $h'(n)\in P$ (if the 
system was not complete, it could be that $h(n)$ was undecidable). Thus $h$ completely represents $A$ under the complete system $\mathcal{S}$.


(a): On the one hand, suppose $h$ defines $A$. Thus (1) $n\in A$ implies $h(n)\in P$, and (2) $n\in\bar{A}$ implies $h(n)\in R$ (which is the same as saying 
$h'(n)\in P$). We need to show that if $h(n)\in P$ implies $n\in A$ and $h(n)\in R$ implies $n\in\bar{A}$. Suppose $h(n)\in P$. 
Thus $h(n)\notin R$ meaning $n\in A$ by (2). If $h(n)\in R$, then $h(n)\notin P$ and thus $n\in\bar{A}$ by (1). Thus $h$ completely represents $A$. On the other hand, 
suppose that $h$ completely represents $A$. It is obvious that $h$ then defines $A$.


(b): Suppose that $h$ contra-represents $A$. Thus $h(n)\in R$ iff $n\in A$, which is the same as saying $h'(n)\in P$ iff $n\in A$, meaning $h'$ represents $A$. 
If $h'$ represents $A$, then $h'(n)\in A$ iff $n\in A$, which is the same as saying $h(n)\in R$ iff $n\in A$. Thus $A$ is contra-representable by $h$.

\section*{Problem 2}
(a): On the one hand, suppose that $h$ represents $A$. Thus $h(n)\in P$ iff $n\in A$, which is the same as saying $r_h(n)\in P$ iff $n\in A$. Because 
$n\in r_h^{-1}(P)$ iff $f(n)\in P$, this means that $n\in r_h^{-1}(P)$ iff $n\in A$. On the other hand, suppose that $A=r_h^{-1}(P)$. Thus 
$n\in A$ iff $r_h(n)\in P$, which is the same as saying $n\in A$ iff $h(n)\in P$. 


(b): Suppose that $h$ contra-represents $A$. Thus $n\in r_h^{-1}(R)$ iff $h(n)\in R$ iff $n\in A$. On the other hand, suppose that $A=r_h^{-1}(R)$. 
Thus $h(n)\in R$ iff $n\in r_h^{-1}(R)$ iff $n\in A$.

\section*{Problem 3}
Suppose that $A$ is represented in $\mathcal{S}$ by $h$, while $\bar{A}$ is not represented by any number. As a consequence of the note in Problem 1, if 
$\mathcal{S}$ was complete, $\bar{A}$ would be represented by $h'$. Thus, because $\bar{A}$ is not represented by $h'$, $\mathcal{S}$ is incomplete. 

\section*{Problem 4}
If $\bar{P^*}$ was representable in $\mathcal{S}$, then there would be some number $h$ such that $h(n)\in P$ iff $n\in\bar{P^*}$. Thus 
$h(h)\in P$ iff $h\in\bar{P^*}$ iff $h(h)\notin P$, which is impossible. Now suppose that $\bar{R^*}$ was representable in $\mathcal{S}$ by $h$. 
Thus $h(n)\in P$ iff $n\in\bar{R^*}$, and $h'(h')\in R$ iff $h(h')\in P$ iff $h'\in\bar{R^*}$ iff $h'(h')\notin R$, which is impossible.

\section*{Problem 5}
(a): Suppose that $h$ represents $R^*$. Thus $h(n)\in P$ iff $n\in R^*$, meaning $h(h)\in P$ iff $h\in R^*$ iff $h(h)\in R$, which means that $h(h)$ is undecidable.


(b): Suppose that $h$ represents $P^*$. Thus $h(n)\in P$ iff $n\in P^*$, meaning $h(h')\in P$ iff $h'\in P^*$ iff $h'(h')\in P$ iff $h(h')\in R$, which means that 
$h(h')$ is undecidable.

\section*{Problem 6}
(a): Suppose that $h$ represents some superset of $R^*$ disjoint from $P^*$. Thus $h(n)\in P$ iff $n\notin P^*$, meaning $h(h)\in P$ iff $h\notin P^*$ iff 
$h(h)\notin P$, meaning $h(h)$ is undecidable.


(b): Suppose that $h$ represents some superset of $P^*$ disjoint from $R^*$. Thus $h(n)\in P$ iff $n\notin R^*$, meaning $h(h')\in P$ iff 
$h'\notin R^*$ iff $h'(h')\notin R$ iff $h(h')\notin P$, meaning $h(h')$ is undecidable.


The reason that these results are stronger than those of Problem 5 is that the results of Problem 5 are special cases of Problem 6. To see this, note that 
$P^*$ is disjoint from $R^*$ for the following reason: $R$ and $P$ are defined to be disjoint, and $h\in P^*$ iff $h(h)\in P$ iff $h(h)\notin R$ iff 
$h\notin R^*$, thus proving that $P^*$ and $R^*$ are disjoint. Thus the results of Problem 5 are special cases of Problem six, taking the superset of $R^*$ disjoint 
from $P^*$ to be $R^*$ itself, and the superset of $P^*$ disjoint from $R^*$ to be $P^*$ itself.

\section*{Problem 7}
Consider any sets $A$ and $B$. Then:


(a): Suppose $A\subseteq B$. Now, $x\in f^{-1}(A)$ iff $f(x)\in A$, and $x\in f^{-1}(B)$ iff $f(x)\in B$. Thus $x\in f^{-1}(A)$ implies $x\in f^{-1}(B)$,  
meaning $f^{-1}(A)\subseteq f^{-1}(B)$.


(b): Suppose that $A$ is disjoint from $B$. Because $x\in f^{-1}(A)$ iff $f(x)\in A$, and $x\in f^{-1}(B)$ iff $f(x)\in B$, $x$ cannot be in both 
$f^{-1}(A)$ and $f^{-1}(B)$, or else $f(x)$ would be in both $A$ and $B$.


(c): We know that $x\in f^{-1}(A)$ iff $f(x)\in A$ and $x\in f^{-1}(\bar{A})$ iff $f(x)\in\bar{A}$, thus meaning 
$x\in f^{-1}(A)$ iff $x\notin f^{-1}(\bar{A})$, therefore meaning they are complements.

\section*{Problem 8}
Suppose that $f(x)$ is admissible. Then:


(a): Suppose that $A$ is representable by $h$. Thus $h(n)\in P$ iff $n\in A$, and there is a number $k$ such that for all for every $n$ $k(n)$ is equivalent to $h(f(n))$. 
Now $x\in f^{-1}(A)$ iff $f(x)\in A$. Thus $k(n)\in P$ iff $h(f(n))\in P$ iff $f(n)\in A$ iff $n\in f^{-1}(A)$, thus proving that $f^{-1}(A)$ is representable by 
$k(n)$. On the other hand, suppose that $A$ is contra-representable by $h$. Thus $h(n)\in R$ iff $n\in A$. Hence $k(n)\in R$ iff $h(f(n))\in R$ iff 
$f(n)\in A$ iff $n\in f^{-1}(A)$, thus proving that $f^{-1}(A)$ is contra-representable by $k(n)$.

(b): Consider some exactly separable disjoint pair $(A,B)$ under some $h$ in $\mathcal{S}$. 
Thus $h$ represents $A$ and contra-represents $B$. Taking $k(n)$ in the same way as in 
part (a), $f^{-1}(A)$ is representable by $k(n)$, while $f^{-1}(B)$ is contra-representable by $k(n)$, thus meaning the pair $(f^{-1}(A),f^{-1}(B))$ is exactly 
separable in $\mathcal{S}$.

\section*{Problem 9}
Suppose that $\mathcal{S}$ is a normal system, which means that for every number $h$, $h^\#(n)$ is equivalent to $h(d(n))$. Now suppose that $A$ is representable by 
some $h$. Thus $h(n)\in P$ iff $n\in A$, and $h^\#(n)\in P$ iff $h(d(n))\in P$ iff $d(n)\in A$. Thus $h\in A^*$ iff $h(h)\in A$ iff $h(d(h))\in P$ iff $h^\#(h)\in P$, 
which means $h^\#$ represents $A^*$ in $\mathcal{S}$.

\section*{Problem 10}
Consider some normal system $\mathcal{S}$, meaning, for every $h$, $h^\#(n)$ is equivalent to $h(d(n))$. 
Now suppose that $\bar{P}$ was representable in $\mathcal{S}$ by $h$. Thus $h(n)\in P$ iff $n\in\bar{P}$. 
Thus $h^\#(h^\#)\in P$ iff $h(h^\#(h^\#))\in P$ iff $h^\#(h^\#)\in\bar{P}$, which is impossible. On the other hand, suppose that $\bar{R}$ was representable 
by $h$. Thus $h(n)\in P$ iff $n\in\bar{R}$. Thus $h^{\#'}(h^{\#'})\in R$ iff 
$h^\#(h^{\#'})\in P$ iff $h(h^{\#'}(h^{\#'}))\in P$ iff $h^{\#'}(h^{\#'})\in\bar{R}$, which is impossible. 


We can also prove this more swiftly. By Problem 9, if $\bar{P}$ is representable, then so is $\bar{P}^*$, which is equal to $\bar{P^*}$ by Problem 7. But by Problem 4, 
$\bar{P^*}$ cannot be representable. Thus $\bar{P}$ cannot be representable. A similar argument shows that $\bar{R}$ is not representable.

\section*{Problem 11}
Consider some normal system $\mathcal{S}$. Thus for every number $h$, $h^\#(n)$ is equivalent to $h(d(n))$. Thus $h(h^\#(h^\#))$ is equivalent to 
$h^\#(h^\#)$, meaning $h^\#(h^\#)$ is a fixed point for $h$.

\section*{Problem 12}
(a): Suppose that $h$ represents $R$, meaning $h(n)\in P$ iff $n\in R$. If $n$ is a fixed point of $h$, then $h(n)\in P$ iff $n\in P$. But $h(n)\in P$ iff 
$n\in R$, meaning $n$ must be undecidable. 


(b): Suppose that $h$ represents $P$, meaning $h(n)\in P$ iff $n\in P$. If $n$ is a fixed point of $h'$, then $n\in P$ iff $h(n)\in P$ iff $h'(n)\in R$ iff $n\in R$, which 
means that $n$ is undecidable. Also, $h'(n)\in P$ iff $n\in P$ iff $h(n)\in P$, thus showing that $h'(n)$ (and $h(n)$) is also undecidable.

\section*{Problem 13}
Suppose that $A$ is some superset of $R^*$ disjoint from $P^*$. If it was definable by some $h$ in $\mathcal{S}$, we would have (1) $n\in A$ implies $h(n)\in P$, and 
(2) $n\in\bar{A}$ implies $h(n)\in R$. Thus $h\in\bar{A}$ implies $h(h)\in R$ meaning $h\in R^*$. But becuse $h\in\bar{A}$, $h\notin R^*$, and thus $h\notin\bar{A}$, that is, 
$h\in A$. Now if $h\notin P^*$, then $h(h)\notin P$, which would imply by (1) that $h\notin A$, a contradiction. Thus $h\in P^*$, contradicting the fact that $A$ was disjoint
from $P^*$, meaning $A$ cannot be definable in $\mathcal{S}$.

\section*{Problem 14}
Note that for all of the following problems, we assume that $\mathcal{S}$ is a standard simple system.

 
Consider some standard simple system $\mathcal{S}$ and any recursive function $f(x)$. Then:


(1') Suppose $A$ is recursively enumerable. Now, $f^{-1}(A)$ is the set of all $x$ such that $f(x)\in A$. Thus $x\in f^{-1}(A)$ iff $\exists y(f(x)=y\wedge y\in A)$, which is recursively 
enumerable.


(2') Suppose that $A$ is recursive. Thus by part (1'), both $f^{-1}(A)$ and $f^{-1}(\bar{A})$ are recursively enumerable. Because $f^{-1}(A)$ and $f^{-1}(\bar{A})$ are complements by 
Problem 7(c), it follows that $f^{-1}(A)$ is recursive.


Note that (1) and (2) of this problem follow by taking $f(x)$ to be $r(x,x)$ ($d(x)$), which is recursive as $\mathcal{S}$ is a standard system.

\section*{Problem 15}
(a): Suppose that $A$ is representable in $\mathcal{S}$ by $h$. Thus, for all $n$, $r(h,n)\in P$ iff $n\in A$. Because $r(h,n)\in P$ is recursively enumerable, so is $A$.


(b): Suppose that $A$ is definable in $\mathcal{S}$ by $h$. Thus (1) $n\in A$ implies $r(h,n)\in P$ and (2) $n\in\bar{A}$ implies $r(h,n)\in R$. These imply that 
$r(h,n)\in P$ implies $n\in A$, and $r(h,n)\in R$ implies $n\in\bar{A}$. Thus $r(h,n)\in P$ and $r(h,n)\in R$ represent $A$ and $\bar{A}$, respectively. By part (a) of this problem, 
both $A$ and $\bar{A}$ are thus recursively enumerable, meaning $A$ is recursive.

\section*{Problem 16}
(a): Suppose that some recursively enumerable but non-recursive set $A$ is representable in $\mathcal{S}$. There are two cases: Either there is some number $n$ outside of $A$, or there 
isn't. Suppose that there was some $n$ outside of $A$. By Problem 15, we know that $A$ cannot be definable in $\mathcal{S}$, or else $A$ would be recursive. Hence, because condition (1) of 
being definable already holds because $A$ is representable, condition (2) must not hold (for the $h$ which represents $A$). Thus $n\in\bar{A}$ does not imply $r(h,n)\in R$. Now, we know that 
there is some $n\in\bar{A}$. Because $r(h,n)\notin R$, it must be undecidable, for being in $P$ would imply that $n\in A$ (because $h$ represents $A$). Thus $r(h,n)$ is undecidable for any $n$ 
outside of $A$. On the other hand, suppose that there were no $n$ outside of $A$. But this is impossible, as that would mean $\bar{A}$ is the empty set, a recursively enumerable set, contrary 
to the assumption that $A$ was not recursive. 


A more swift proof can be seen in this way: Because $A$ is not recursive, $\bar{A}$ is not recursively enumerable, and hence not representable (because all representable sets in $\mathcal{S}$ 
are recursively enumerable by Problem 15). Thus, because $A$ is represented in $\mathcal{S}$, but not $\bar{A}$, $\mathcal{S}$ is incomplete by Problem 3. 

(b): Suppose that all recursively enumerable sets are representable in $\mathcal{S}$. Then there must be some recursively enumerable non-recursive set representable in $\mathcal{S}$. For 
example, the set $T_0$ of the Gödel numbers of the true sentences of the system $(U)$ defined in chapter 4 is one such set. Hence, by part (a) of this problem, $\mathcal{S}$ is incomplete.

\section*{Problem 17}
Suppose that $\mathcal{S}$ is undecidable and standard. Thus both $P$ and $R$ are recursively enumerable. So if there was not some number outside of both $P$ and $R$, $R$ would be the 
complement of $P$, meaning $P$ would be recursive, a contradiction to the assumed undecidability. Hence there must be some number outside of both $P$ and $R$, meaning $\mathcal{S}$ is 
incomplete. 

\section*{Problem 18}
Suppose that all recursive sets are representable in $\mathcal{S}$. Now, suppose that $\mathcal{S}$ was not undecidable, which would mean that $P$ is recursive. By Problem 
14(1), $P^*$ is also recursive. Hence $\bar{P^*}$ is also recursive, and therefore representable in $\mathcal{S}$ by some $h$. Thus, for all $n$, $h(n)\in P$ iff $n\in\bar{P^*}$. 
This means $h(h)\notin P$ iff $h\in P^*$ iff $h(h)\in P$, which is impossible. Thus $\mathcal{S}$ being decidable raises a contradiction, therefore meaning it must be undecidable.

\section*{Problem 19}
Suppose that the pair $(P,R)$ is recursively inseparable. Hence there is no recursive superset of $P$ disjoint from $R$. So if $\mathcal{S}$ was not undecidable, $P$ would be recursive. Because 
$P$ is a superset of itself, this is a contradiction to the assumed recursive inseparability. Thus $\mathcal{S}$ must be undecidable.

\section*{Problem 20}
Consider any recursive function $f(x)$ and any disjoint pair $(A,B)$. Suppose $(f^{-1}(A),f^{-1}(B))$ is recursively inseparable. Hence there is no recursive superset of $f^{-1}(A)$ disjoint from 
$f^{-1}(B)$. Now suppose that $(A,B)$ was not recursively inseparable. Hence some superset $S$ of $A$ disjoint from $B$ is recursive. But $x\in f^{-1}(S)$ iff $f(x)\in S$. Thus 
$f^{-1}(S)$ is equivalent to $f(x)\in S$, meaning $f^{-1}(S)$ is recursive. Now, $f^{-1}(S)$ is a supserset of $f^{-1}(A)$, as $x\in f^{-1}(A)$ iff $f(x)\in A$, which would mean $f(x)\in S$ iff 
$x\in f^{-1}(S)$. Also, $f^{-1}(S)$ is disjoint from $f^{-1}(B)$, as $x\in f^{-1}(B)$ iff $f(x)\in B$, which would mean $f(x)\notin S$ iff $x\notin f^{-1}(S)$.
Thus, because $f^{-1}(S)$ is a superset of $f^{-1}(A)$ disjoint from $f^{-1}(B)$, this is contrary to the 
assumption of recursive inseparability for $(f^{-1}(A),f^{-1}(B))$. Thus $(A,B)$ must be recursively inseparable. Thus if $(P^*,R^*)$ is recursively inseparable, so is $(P,R)$, as 
$P^*=d^{-1}(P)$ and $R^*=d^{-1}(R)$.

\section*{Problem 21}
Suppose that every recursive set is definable in $\mathcal{S}$. Suppose that $(P^*,R^*)$ was not recursively inseparable. Hence there would be some recurisve 
superset of $P^*$ disjoint from $R^*$, and because the superset is recursive, it would be definable in $\mathcal{S}$. 
But this is impossible by Problem 13. Thus $(P^*,R^*)$ is recursively inseparable, meaning so is $(P,R)$ by Problem 20.

\section*{Problem 22}
Consider some standard simple system $\mathcal{S}$, and suppose that some disjoint pair $(A_1,A_2)$ is recursively inseparable, but separable in $\mathcal{S}$. Thus some number $h$ represents 
some superset $S_1$ of $A_1$ and contra-represents some superset $S_2$ of $A_2$. Suppose that $S_1$ and $S_2$ were not disjoint, that is, they shared some number $n$. Thus $h(n)\in P$ and 
$h(n)\in R$, which is impossible. Therefore $S_1$ and $S_2$ are disjoint. Because $(A_1,A_2)$ is recursively inseparable, so is $(S_1,S_2)$. For if it weren't, there would be some recursive 
superset $S$ of $S_1$ disjoint from $S_2$. But $S$ would also be a superset of $A_1$, and because it is disjoint from $S_2$, would also be disjoint from $A_2$, in contradiction to the assumed 
recursive inseparability of $(A_1,A_2)$. Thus $(S_1,S_2)$ is recursively inseparable. Now, 
$S_1=r_h^{-1}(P)$ and $S_2=r_h^{-1}(R)$ by Problem 2. Thus the pair $(r_h^{-1}(P),r_h^{-1}(R))$ is recursively inseparable, meaning so is $(P,R)$ by Problem 20.


Corollary 1 follows in this way: Suppose some recursively inseparable pair is separable in $\mathcal{S}$. By Theorem 4, $(P,R)$ is recursively inseparable. Thus by Problem 19, $\mathcal{S}$ 
is undecidable. 


Corollary 2 follows in this way: Suppose some recursively inseparable pair is separable in $\mathcal{S}$. Thus $\mathcal{S}$ is undecidable by Corollary 1, and is thus also incomplete by 
Theorem 1.

\section*{Problem 23}

Suppose $\mathcal{S}$ is a Rosser system. Thus for any two recursively enumerable sets $A$ and $B$, there is a number $h$ that separates $A-B$ from $B-A$. Consider any completely effectively inseparable pair of

Now, because $K_1$ and $K_2$ are disjoint, $K_1-K_2=K_1$ and $K_2-K_1=K_2$. Thus, because $\mathcal{S}$ is a Rosser system, $K_1$ is separable from $K_2$ in $\mathcal{S}$ by some number $h$. Thus $h$ represents

(1) $x\in A$ iff $f(x)\in U_1$;\newline
(2) $x\in B$ iff $f(x)\in U_2$;\newline
Now, we know that $f(x)$ is admissible in $\mathcal{S}$ (because it only has one argument). Consider the $h$ that exactly separates $(U_1,U_2)$. Thus there is some number $k$ such that for every $n$ the

$(A,B)$ is exactly separable by the number $k$. 

\section*{Problem 24}

On the other hand, suppose that $R_2(n,\Pi(a,b))\wedge\oldneg R_1(n,\Pi(a,b))$ holds.

$(1')$ $R_1(n,h)\wedge\oldneg R_2(n,h)$ implies $h(n)\in P$;\newline
$(2')$ $R_2(n,h)\wedge\oldneg R_1(n,h)$ implies $h(n)\in R$;\newline
when taking $\Pi(a,b)$ for $h$. 

\section*{Problem 25}
Suppose that $\mathcal{S}$ is an effective Rosser system. Consider any two recursively enumerable disjoint sets $A$ and $B$. Recall that $y(x)=r(y,x)$, a recursive function. 
Let $R_1(x,y)$ be the relation $x\in A\vee y(x)\in R$ and $R_2(x,y)$ be the relation $x\in B\vee y(x)\in P$, which are both recursively enumerable. By Proposition 3, there is a number 
$h$ such that for all $n$:\newline
(1) $R_1(n,h)\wedge\oldneg R_2(n,h)$ implies $h(n)\in P$;\newline
(2) $R_2(n,h)\wedge\oldneg R_1(n,h)$ implies $h(n)\in R$;\newline
that is:\newline
$(1')$ $(n\in A\vee h(n)\in R)\wedge\oldneg (n\in B\vee h(n)\in P)$ implies $h(n)\in P$;\newline
$(2')$ $(n\in B\vee h(n)\in P)\wedge\oldneg (n\in A\vee h(n)\in R)$ implies $h(n)\in R$.\newline
We must show that $h$ exactly separates $A$ from $B$, that is, $n\in A$ iff $h(n)\in P$ and $n\in B$ iff $h(n)\in R$. 


First, suppose $h(n)\in P$. Thus, by $(2')$, $\oldneg((n\in B\vee h(n)\in P)\wedge\oldneg (n\in A\vee h(n)\in R))$ holds. $\oldneg(n\in B\vee h(n)\in P)$ cannot hold, or else $h(n)\notin P$, 
a contradiction. Thus $n\in A\vee h(n)\in R$ holds, and because $h(n)\in R$ cannot hold, we have $h(n)\in A$. On the other hand, suppose that $h(n)\in R$. 
Thus, by $(1')$, $\oldneg((n\in A\vee h(n)\in R)\wedge\oldneg (n\in B\vee h(n)\in P))$ holds. It cannot be that $\oldneg(n\in A\vee h(n)\in R)$ holds, or else $h(n)\notin R$, a contradiction.
Thus $n\in B\vee h(n)\in P$ holds. But because $h(n)\in P$ cannot hold, $n\in B$.


Now we show the converses. First suppose that $n\in A$. Thus $n\in A\vee h(n)\in R$ holds, and $h(n)\notin R$, or else $n\in B$ by the above paragraph (and $A$ and $B$ are disjoint). 
Now, if $h(n)\notin P$, we would have $h(n)$ being undecidable. But because $n\notin B$ and $h(n)\notin P$, we would have $\oldneg (n\in B\vee h(n)\in P)$ holding. Thus 
$(n\in A\vee h(n)\in R)\wedge\oldneg (n\in B\vee h(n)\in P)$ holds, meaning $h(n)\in P$, a contradiction. Hence $h(n)$ cannot be undecidable, and consequently $h(n)\in P$. A similar argument 
proves that if $n\in B$, then $h(n)\in R$. 


Thus we have shown that $n\in A$ iff $h(n)\in P$ and $n\in B$ iff $h(n)\in R$, meaning $h$ exactly separates $A$ from $B$. Thus we have shown, for any two disjoint recursively enumerable sets $A$ 
and $B$, there is some number $h$ that exactly separates $A$ from $B$, meaning, by definition, $\mathcal{S}$ is an exact Rosser system.

\section*{Problem 27}
Consider some effective Rosser system, and any recursively enumerable sequence of extensions of $\mathcal{S}$. Thus the relations $x\in P_y$ and $x\in R_y$ are both recursively enumerable. By the iteration theorem, 
there are recursive functions $f_1(x)$ and $f_2(x)$ such that $\omega_{f_1(n)}=\{x: x\in R_n\}$ and $\omega_{f_2(n)}=\{x: x\in P_n\}$. That is, $\omega_{f_1(n)}=R_n$ and $\omega_{f_2(n)}=P_n$. 
Then, by the Rosser fixed point property, there is a number $h$ such that for all $n$ (because $\omega_{f_1(n)}$, that is $R_n$, is always disjoint from $\omega_{f_2(n)}$, that is $P_n$):\newline
(1) $h(n)\in\omega_{f_1(n)}$ implies $h(n)\in P$;\newline
(2) $h(n)\in\omega_{f_2(n)}$ implies $h(n)\in R$.\newline 
Now consider any $n$, and suppose that $h(n)\in P_n$, meaning $h(n)\in\omega_{f_2(n)}$, and thus, by (2), $h(n)\in R$. 
Recall that, because $\mathcal{S}_n$ is an extension of $\mathcal{S}$, $P\subseteq P_n$ and $R\subseteq R_n$. Thus $h(n)\in R_n$, a contradiction. Hence $h(n)\notin P_n$. Similar reasoning can be used to show that 
$h(n)\notin R_n$. Thus we have shown, for every $n$, $h(n)\notin P_n\cup R_n$, which proves that $\mathcal{S}$ is uniformly incompletable.
