\section*{Problem 1}
An expression $t$ is a term iff there is some sequence $t_1,...,t_n$ such that every $t_i$ is either a variable or a constant, or is an expression of the form 
$(t_jt_k)$ for some $j<i$ and $k<i$, and such that there is some sequence $k_1,...,k_m$ such that every $k_i$ is some $t_i$ and 
$k_1...k_m=t$. Smullyan's answer seems to be incorrect here. For even consider the term $(x_1x_2)$. According to his answer for Problem 1, we need to find a finite sequence 
$t_1...t_n=(x_1x_2)$ such that for each $i\le n$, either $t_i$ is a variable or constant, or there are numbers $j_1$ and $j_2$ both less than $i$ such that 
$t_i=(t_{j1}t_{j2})$. But such a sequence cannot be found. We would need to have some $t_i=(x_1x_2)$. Because this is neither a constant nor a variable, we would need 
to have $j_1$ and $j_2$ such that $(x_1x_2)=(t_{j1}t_{j2})$ for $j_1<i$ and $j_2<i$. Thus the sequence would be of the form
$x_1x_2(x_1x_2)$. But $x_1x_2(x_1x_2)\ne (x_1x_2)$. Thus this definition does not work. Instead, with the preceeding definition, one could construct the sequence 
$t_1t_2t_3=x_1x_2(x_1x_2)$ and from that construct the sequence $k_1=(x_1x_2)$, which would show that $(x_1x_2)$ is a term.

\section*{Justification for Term Induction}
Here is why term induction can be derived from mathematical induction. Now, suppose $S$ is a set whose members are variables or constants or both. 
Let $S^+$ be the set of all terms built from elements of $S$ (and parantheses). Now suppose that a property $P$ holds for all elements of $S$, and if any terms 
$t_1$ and $t_2$ have property $P$, then so does $(t_1t_2)$. Now, $S^+$ consists of all variables and constants in $S$, in fact, all elements of $S$. We wish to show that 
for all $n$ being the number of occurrences of elements of $S$ in a term in $S^+$, that term has property $P$. The base case is obvious, as terms having only 1 occurence are 
either variables or constants, and these are assumed to have property $P$ (as they are all in $S$). Now suppose the assumption holds for all number $<n$. A term having 
$n$ occurences of elements of $S$ must be of the form $(t_1t_2)$ where $t_1,t_2$ both have less than $n$ occurences of elements of $S$ in them. Hence both 
$t_1$ and $t_2$ have property $P$, meaning so does $(t_1t_2)$, that is, $t$. Thus we have proven by complete induction that all elements of $S^+$ have the property $P$.

\section*{Problem 2}
(i): Suppose that $\mathcal{W}$ is $x$. Hence $\mathcal{W}_x$ is $I$. (1) and (2) hold, as $I$ contains no variables. Also, $\mathcal{W}_xx=Ix=x=\mathcal{W}$, 
meaning condition (3) holds. Hence $I$ is an $x$-eliminate of $\mathcal{W}$.


(ii): Suppose that $\mathcal{W}$ is any term in which $x$ does not occur. Hence $\mathcal{W}_x$ is $K\mathcal{W}$. (1) holds, as 
$K\mathcal{W}$ contains only those variables in $\mathcal{W}$, and $\mathcal{W}$ does not contain $x$; this also means (2) holds. 
(3) also holds, as $K\mathcal{W}x=\mathcal{W}$. Thus $K\mathcal{W}$ is an $x$-eliminate of $\mathcal{W}$.


(iii): Suppose that $\mathcal{W}_x$ and $\mathcal{V}_x$ are $x$-eliminates of $\mathcal{W}$ and $\mathcal{V}$, respectively. Thus 
$(\mathcal{W}\mathcal{V})_x$ is $S\mathcal{W}_x\mathcal{V}_x$. (1) holds, as $x$ was assumed to not occur in either of $\mathcal{W}_x$ or $\mathcal{V}_x$. 
(2) holds in the following way: The variables of $S\mathcal{W}_x\mathcal{V}_x$ are those of $\mathcal{W}$ and $\mathcal{V}$. The variables in 
$(\mathcal{W}\mathcal{V})$ are those in $\mathcal{W}$ or in $\mathcal{V}$. 
Because we assumed the variables of $\mathcal{W}_x$ and $\mathcal{V}_x$ occured in $\mathcal{W}$ and $\mathcal{V}$, respectively, it thus 
follows that the variables of $S\mathcal{W}_x\mathcal{V}_x$ are all in $(\mathcal{W}\mathcal{V})$. (3) also holds, as 
$S\mathcal{W}_x\mathcal{V}_xx=\mathcal{W}_xx(\mathcal{V}x)=\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{V})=\mathcal{W}\mathcal{V}$. Thus $S\mathcal{W}_x\mathcal{V}_x$ is an $x$-eliminate of 
$(\mathcal{W}\mathcal{V})$.


Note that this is why Proposition 1 follows from Problem 2 by term induction. We wish $S^+$ to be the set of all simple terms. Thus let $S$ be the set of all variables, as 
well as the constants $S,K$ and $I$. Hence $S^+$ is the set of all terms built from elements of $S$ and parentheses, that is, all simple terms. Let $P$ be the property that 
$\mathcal{W}_x$ is an $x$-eliminate of $\mathcal{W}$. We have shown by (i) that 
$\mathcal{W}_x$ for $\mathcal{W}$ being a variable is an $x$-eliminate of $\mathcal{W}$. Similarly, for $S,K$ and $I$, we showed in (ii) that 
their respective $\mathcal{W}_x$'s are $x$-eliminates of them. Thus we have shown that $P$ holds for all elements of $S$. Now suppose we have any terms $t_1$ and $t_2$ 
of $S^+$ such that $t_1$ and $t_2$ have the property $P$. Because $t_1$ and $t_2$ are simple terms, we thus showed in (iii) that $(t_1t_2)$ also has property $P$. 
Thus, by term induction, $P$ holds for all elements in $S^+$, which is what we were required to prove. 

\section*{Problem 3}
Suppose that $x$ does not occur in $\mathcal{W}$. We prove that $\mathcal{W}$ is an $x$-eliminate of $\mathcal{W}x$. (1) holds, as 
$x$ was assumed to not occur in $\mathcal{W}$. (2) obviously holds, as $\mathcal{W}$ is itself part of $\mathcal{W}x$. (3) also obviously holds, as 
$\mathcal{W}_xx=\mathcal{W}x=\mathcal{W}x$.

\section*{Problem 4}
(2) is the one which is true. For consider $\mathcal{W}_{x,y}$. (1) and (2) obviously hold. Because $\mathcal{W}_{x,y}=(\mathcal{W}_x)_y$, we have
$\mathcal{W}_{x,y}yx=(\mathcal{W}_x)_yyx=\mathcal{W}_xx=\mathcal{W}$, meaning (3) also holds.


Here is an explicit proof of Proposition 2 from Problem 4 and mathematical induction. The base cases of Proposition 2, namely when $n\le 2$, are just Problems 2 and 4. 
Now suppose the result holds for some $n$, that is, $\mathcal{W}_{x_nx_{n-1}...x_1}$ is an $x_1,x_2,...,x_n$ eliminate of $\mathcal{W}$. Now, 
$\mathcal{W}_{x_{n+1}x_n...x_1}$ is $(...(\mathcal{W}_{x_{n+1}})_{x_n})...)_{x_1})$. Conditions (1) and (2) obviously hold. As for (3), 
\[ \mathcal{W}_{x_{n+1}x_n...x_1}x_1x_2...x_{n+1}=(...(\mathcal{W}_{x_{n+1}})_{x_n})...)_{x_1})x_1x_2...x_{n+1} \]
\[ =(...(\mathcal{W}_{x_{n+1}})_{x_n})...)_{x_2}x_2...x_{n+1} \]
\[ =(...(\mathcal{W}_{x_{n+1}})_{x_n})...)_{x_3}x_3...x_{n+1} \]
\[ ... \]
\[ =\mathcal{W}_{x_{n+1}}x_{n+1}=\mathcal{W} \]

\section*{Problem 5}
(1): We can prove this in two ways. First, suppose that $A$ is the combinator which realizes the term $t(x,x_1,...,x_n)$. Because we are working in a complete system, 
we can consider the fixed point $A'$ of $A$. Take $\Gamma$ to be $AA'$ (or, equivalently, just $A'$), 
as $AA'x_1...x_n=t(A',x_1,...,x_n)=t(AA'(x_1,...,x_n))$. An alternative proof instead supposes that 
$A$ is the combinator which realizes the term $t((xx),x_1,...,x_n)$. In this case, we take $\Gamma$ to be $AA$,
as $AAx_1,...,x_n=t(AA,x_1,...,x_n)$.


\textit{Note to self}: Someone could ask: \textquote{We have already proved that there is some combinator that realizes every simple term $t$. So, if we have a given 
simple term $t(x,x_1,...,x_n)$, we could, for \textit{any} combinator $A$, take $t_1$ to be $t(A,x_1,...,x_n)$, and then there is some combinator $B$ which realizes 
$t_1$. Doesn't this prove the fixed point theorem?} It shows that there is, for each combinator $A$, \textit{some other} combinator $B$ that realizes 
$t(A,x_1,...,x_n)$. But this means $B$ could be something other than $A$. The theorem wants to have $A$ and $B$ be the same, so this line of reasoning does not work.  


(2): Part (a) of the exercise is a special case when $t(x_1,x,y)$ is the term $x(x_1y)$ (this is just the queer bird $Q$, as $Qxyz=y(xz)$), 
as then $\Gamma xy=x(\Gamma y)$. Part (b) of the exercise follows when 
$t(x_1,x,y)$ is the term $x_1yx$ (this is just the cardinal $C$, which was defined as $Cxyz=xzy$), as then $\Gamma xy=\Gamma yx$.

\section*{Problem 6}
Suppose the terms $t_1(x,x_1,...,x_n)$ and $t_2(x,x_1,...,x_n)$ are realizable by $A_1$ and $A_2$, respectively. Because the system is complete, we have the combinator 
$A_1(A_2(BA_1A_2)')$ for $(BA_1A_2)'$ the fixed point of $(BA_1A_2)$. Take $\Gamma_1$ to be $A_1(A_2(BA_1A_2)')$, and $\Gamma_2$ to be 
$A_2(BA_1A_2)'$. Then:
\[ A_1(A_2(BA_1A_2)')x_1...x_n=t_1(A_2(BA_1A_2)',x_1,...,x_n) \] and 
\[ A_2(BA_1A_2)'x_1....x_n=t_2((BA_1A_2)',x_1,...,x_n) \]
\[ =t_2(BA_1A_2(BA_1A_2)',x_1,...,x_n) \]
\[ =t_2(A_1(A_2(BA_1A_2)'),x_1,...,x_n) \]
That is:
\[ \Gamma_1x_1...x_n=t_1(\Gamma_2,x_1,...,x_n) \]
\[ \Gamma_2x_1...x_n=t_2(\Gamma_1,x_1,...,x_n) \]


Smullyan provides two alternative solutions. One takes $A_1$ and $A_2$ to be the combinators which realize 
$t_1(xyx,x_1,...,x_n)$ and $t_2(xyx,x_1,...,x_n)$, respectively. That is:
\[ A_1xyx_1...x_n=t_1(xyx,x_1,...,x_n) \] \[ A_2xyx_1...x_n=t_2(xyx,x_1,...,x_n) \] and then the theorem is satisfied by taking $\Gamma_1$ to be 
$A_1A_2A_1$ and $\Gamma_2$ to be $A_2A_1A_2$, as:
\[ A_1A_2A_1=t_1(A_2A_1A_2,x_1,...,x_n) \]
\[ A_2A_1A_2=t_2(A_1A_2A_1,x_1,...,x_n) \]


The other proof uses the previously introduced notion of a cross point $(x,y)$ of a pair $(A,B)$ meaning $Ax=y$ and $By=x$. By Problem 6 of Chapter 8, and 
the fact that we are working in a complete system, every pair of terms has a cross point. Thus, if $A_1$ and $A_2$ realize $t_1$ and $t_2$ respectively, we let
$(\Gamma_1,\Gamma_2)$ be the cross point of $(A_2,A_1)$, as then $A_2\Gamma_1=\Gamma_2$ and $A_1\Gamma_2=\Gamma_1$, meaning:
\[ \Gamma_1x_1...x_n=A_1\Gamma_2x_1...x_n=t_1(\Gamma_2,x_1,...,x_n) \]
\[ \Gamma_2x_1...x_n=A_2\Gamma_1x_1...x_n=t_2(\Gamma_1,x_1,...,x_n) \] 

\section*{Problem 7}
We wish to prove Proposition 3. This can be done by using term induction. Take the set $S$ to be all of the variables, as well as $S,B,C$ and $I$ (the only constants in 
our system). Hence $S^+$ is the set of all nice terms. Now, let $P$ be the property that, if the term $\mathcal{W}$ is nice and such that $x$ occurs in it, then 
$\mathcal{W}_x$ is a nice-$x$-eliminate of $\mathcal{W}$. We need to first show that $P$ holds for all elements of $S$, and then show that it holds for all elements of 
$S^+$. Well, for every element of $S$, it either is $x$ or it 
isn't. If it isn't, the property vacuosly holds. If it is $x$, then $\mathcal{W}_x$ is $I$. Recall the conditions of being a nice-$x$-eliminate of $\mathcal{W}$:\newline
(1) The variable $x$ does not occur in $\mathcal{W}_x$.\newline
(2) All variables of $\mathcal{W}_x$ occur in $\mathcal{W}$.\newline
(3) The equation $\mathcal{W}_xx=\mathcal{W}$ holds.\newline
Because $I$ has no variables, conditions (1) and (2) obviously hold. (3) also holds, as $Ix=x=\mathcal{W}$. Thus $I$ is an nice-$x$-eliminate of 
$x$. We have therefore shown that $P$ holds for all elements in $S$. Now consider any terms $t_1$ and $t_2$ of $S^+$. Suppose $t_1$ and $t_2$ have property $P$. 
Consider the term $(t_1t_2)$. There are now multiple cases.


In the first case, $x$ occurs both in $t_1$ and $t_2$. Thus $(t_1t_2)_x$ is $S(t_1)_x(t_2)_x$. Conditions (1) and (2) obviously hold (by the assumption of 
$t_1$ and $t_2$ having property $P$). (3) also holds, as $S(t_1)_x(t_2)_xx=(t_1)_xx((t_2)_xx)=t_1(t_2)=t_1t_2$.


In the second case, $x$ occurs in $t_1$ but not in $t_2$. Thus $(t_1t_2)_x$ is $C(t_1)_xt_2$. Conditions (1) and (2) obviously hold. (3) also holds, as 
$C(t_1)_xt_2x=(t_1)_xxt_2=t_1t_2$.


In the third case, $x$ occurs in $t_2$ but not in $t_1$. There are then two sub-cases. In the first, $t_2$ is just $x$. Then $(t_1t_2)_x$ is $t_1$. 
Again, conditions (1) and (2) obviously hold. As for (3), it also holds, as $t_1x=t_1t_2$ (as we assumed $t_2$ was just $x$). In the second sub-case, 
$t_2\ne x$, but $x$ occurs in $t_2$ but not in $t_1$. Then $(t_1t_2)_x$ is $Bt_1(t_2)_x$. Again, (1) and (2) obviously hold. As for (3), it also holds, as
$Bt_1(t_2)_xx=t_1((t_2)_xx)=t_1(t_2)=t_1t_2$.


The last case is the vacuous one, in which neither $t_1$ or $t_2$ contain $x$. Then $(t_1t_2)$ would also not contain $x$, meaning $P$ would hold vacuously for it.


Thus, in all cases, $(t_1t_2)$ also has the property $P$. Then, by term induction, all elements of $S^+$ have the property $P$. As a consequence of this, 
for any nice term $\mathcal{W}$ in which $x$ occurs, $\mathcal{W}_x$ is a nice-$x$-eliminate of $\mathcal{W}$.


Here is the proof of the generalization of this result: Suppose that the variables $x_1,...,x_n$ occur in some nice term $\mathcal{W}$. We then must show that 
$\mathcal{W}_{x_nx_{n-1}...x_1}$ is a nice $x_1,...,x_n$ eliminate of $\mathcal{W}$. Recall that 
$\mathcal{W}_{x_nx_{n-1}...x_1}$ is an abbreviation for $(...(\mathcal{W}_{x_n})_{x_{n-1}})...)_{x_1})$. 
This can be proven by induction on $n$. The base case is just the above result, when 
we showed that for any nice term $\mathcal{W}$ in which $x$ occurs, the term $\mathcal{W}_x$ is a nice $x$-eliminate of $\mathcal{W}$. Now consider any $n>1$. 
Now, conditions (1) and (2) obviously hold for $\mathcal{W}_{x_{n+1}x_n...x_1}$. As for (3),
\[ \mathcal{W}_{x_{n+1}x_n...x_1}x_1x_2...x_{n+1}=(...(\mathcal{W}_{x_{n+1}})x_n)...)x_1)x_1x_2...x_{n+1} \]
\[ =(...(\mathcal{W}_{x_{n+1}})_{x_n})...)x_2x_2...x_{n+1} \]
\[ =(...(\mathcal{W}_{x_{n+1}})_{x_n})...)x_3x_3...x_{n+1} \]
\[ =\mathcal{W}_{x_{n+1}}x_{n+1} \]
\[ =\mathcal{W} \]

\section*{Problem 8}
(a): Take $Q_3$ to be $GI$, as $GIxyz=Iz(xy)=z(xy)$.


(b): Take $C$ to be $GGII$, as $GGIIxyz=Gx(II)yz=xz(IIy)=xzy$.


(c): Take $T$ to be $CI$, as $CIxy=Iyx=yx$. Alternatively, we could take $T$ to be $Q_3I$, as $Q_3Ixy=y(Ix)=y(x)=yx$.


(d): Recall that we can take $R$ to be $CC$, as $CCxyz=Cyxz=yzx$. Then take $Q$ to be $C(Q_3Q_3)C$, as 
$C(Q_3Q_3)Cxyz=Q_3Q_3xCyz=C(Q_3x)yz=Q_3xzy=y(xz)$. Alternatively, we could take $Q$ to be $RC(Q_3Q_3)$, as

$GRQ_3xyz=Ry(Q_3x)z=Q_3xzy=y(xz)$. Then, take $B$ to be $CQ$, as $CQxyz=Qyxz=x(yz)$.

\section*{Derivations of and from $J$ and $I$}
Here are derivations for $B,T$ and $M$ from $J$ and $I$. We can easily see that $JII$ is $T$, as:
\[ JIIxy=II(Iyx)=Iyx=yx \] Then, we can take $R$ to be $JT$, as $JTxyz=Tx(Tzy)=Tzyx=yzx$, and we can thus take $C$ to be $RRR$, that is, 
$JT(JT)(JT)$. We then take $Q_2$ to be $C(JI)$, as $C(JI)xyz=JIyxz=Iy(Izx)=y(zx)$. Then it follows that $Q_1$ is also present, as 
$CQ_2xyz=Q_2yxz=x(zy)$. Then we also have $Q_4$, as $Q_1Txyz=T(yx)z=z(yx)$. Then we also have $Q_3$, in particular, from $CQ_4$, as
$CQ_4xyz=Q_4yxz=z(xy)$ (the derivations for $Q_3$ and $Q_4$ are not necessary). 


Next, we can derive $B$. Take $B$ to be $C(JIC)Q_1$, as \[ C(JIC)Q_1xyz=JICxQ_1yz=IC(IQ_1x)yz=C(Q_1x)yz=Q_1xzy=x(yz) \]
Now having derived $B$ and $T$, it follows from problems in Chapter 9 that we have the vireo $V$ (which we recall was defined by the condition $Vxyz=zxy$), 
and also $V^*$. We can then take our mocker $M$ to be 
$V^*(B(CJI)T)IT$, as \[ V^*(B(CJI)T)ITx=B(CJI)TxIT=CJI(Tx)IT=J(Tx)IIT=TxI(TxTI) \] \[ =Ix(TxI)=x(Ix)=x(x)=xx \]
Thus, we have derived $T,B$ and $M$ (in that order) from $J$ and $I$.


In \textit{To Mock a Mockingbird}, Smullyan provides alternative derivations for $B$ and $M$. He first derives $Q_1,T$ and $R$ all in the same manner as above. Then he 
takes $C^*$ to be $C(Q_1C)$, as $C(Q_1C)xyzw=Q_1Cyxzw=C(xy)zw=xywz$. After that, he takes $B$ to be $C^*Q_1$, as 
$C^*Q_1xyz=Q_1xzy=x(yz)$. He then defines a new bird $J_1$ defined by $J_1xyzw=yx(wxz)$, which he takes to be $BJT$, as
$BJTxyzw=J(Tx)yzw=Txy(Txwz)=yx(wxz)$. Then he notes that $J_1xTTT=Tx(TxT)=TxTx=Txx=xx$. In turn,
$J_1xTTT=CJ_1TxTT=C(CJ_1T)TxT=C(C(CJ_1T)T)Tx$. Hence, he takes $M$ to be $C(C(CJ_1T)T)T$. 


It is also true that we can derive $J$ from $B,T$ and $M$. Because we have $B,T$ and $M$, we have, by problems in Chapter 9, $W$, $C^{***}$, $C^*$, and $E$. 
Then take $J$ to be $W(C^{***}(C^*E))$, as \[ W(C^{***}(C^*E))xyzw=C^{***}(C^*E)xxyzw=C^*Exxywz=Exyxwz=xy(xwz) \] In \textit{To Mock a Mockingbird}, Smullyan 
provides a slightly different derivation of $J$. He takes it to be $C^{**}(W(C^*E))$, as
$C^{**}(W(C^*E))xyzw=W(C^*E)xywz=C^*Exxywz=Exyxwz=xy(xwz)$.
